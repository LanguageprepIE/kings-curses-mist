## 1. The River's Apprentice

I've walked beside the Boyne in every season, yet the river never seems to tell quite the same story twice. Once, near a bend where the reeds grow thick, I heard of a boy who came seeking wisdom and a poet who had spent seven years waiting for it. People still disagree about some details. They agree on the boy's name: Fionn.

He arrived at Finegas's small riverside dwelling on a misty morning, tired from the road but determined to become the poet's pupil.

Finegas was not a poet of the kind who merely found pleasing words for a song. He knew the histories of kings, the names of distant places and the laws by which people settled their quarrels. He could look at a tree and tell what season had shaped its branches. He could listen to a stranger and hear what the stranger had left unsaid.

Fionn wanted to learn from him.

“Why?” Finegas asked when the boy appeared at his door.

Fionn had prepared several answers. He wanted to understand the world. He wanted to become wise. He wanted to know things that other people did not. But the old poet watched him so closely that the answers suddenly sounded foolish.

“Because I have questions,” Fionn said at last.

Finegas raised an eyebrow. “Most people do.”

“I keep finding more.”

For the first time, the poet smiled. He gave Fionn a place by the hearth and work to do. In return, he would teach him.

Their days soon found a rhythm. Fionn fetched water, gathered wood and tended the fire. He listened while Finegas recited verses and repeated them until he could hear their pattern. He learned the names of birds by their calls, and how the same river could look silver in the morning and black beneath a storm.

Sometimes he asked so many questions that Finegas put down his work and closed his eyes.

“Must you know everything today?”

“No,” Fionn replied. “Tomorrow will do.”

The poet laughed in spite of himself.

There was one thing, however, that Finegas seldom laughed about: a deep pool in the Boyne, where he spent hours watching the water.

## 2. Seven Years

One evening Fionn found his master sitting beside the pool long after the light had begun to fade.

“Are you waiting for someone?” he asked.

“For something.”

“A fish?”

Finegas glanced at him. “Not an ordinary fish.”

He told Fionn of a salmon unlike any other in Ireland, a creature said to possess extraordinary knowledge. Some storytellers linked its gift to sacred hazels and the mysteries of the river. Others simply called it the Salmon of Knowledge, as though its name explained enough.

For seven years Finegas had watched this pool. He had come through spring floods and winter frost, through days when rain blurred the banks and days when the water lay still as polished stone. A prophecy had promised that one named Fionn would taste the salmon and receive its gift.

Fionn looked at his master. “But that is my name.”

Finegas was quiet for a moment.

“Names,” he said, “can be troublesome things.”

Then he returned his gaze to the dark water, and Fionn understood that the poet had waited too long to speak lightly of this.

The boy began to watch the pool with him. At first he expected some sign of enchantment: a light beneath the surface, perhaps, or a voice rising from the current. But the Boyne offered only its familiar sounds. Water moved over stones. Midges gathered in the last warmth of evening. A heron stood motionless among the reeds.

“Perhaps it has gone elsewhere,” Fionn suggested after several days.

“Perhaps.”

“Perhaps it was never here.”

Finegas did not answer.

Fionn regretted the words. He was beginning to understand that seven years was a long time to give to a hope, even for a man who knew so much.

Then, on a day that began like any other, the line in Finegas's hands pulled suddenly taut.

The poet rose so quickly that his stool overturned. The line cut a narrow path across the water. Something powerful turned beneath the surface, stirring the dark pool into silver circles.

Fionn ran to help, but Finegas called for him to stand back.

For a long while neither man nor fish would yield. Then the salmon broke the surface. Its scales flashed like wet metal in the sunlight, and for an instant it seemed too bright to belong to the river.

At last Finegas drew it ashore.

He knelt beside the fish, breathing hard. The years of waiting were over. Yet his face showed not triumph but astonishment, as though he hardly trusted the sight before him.

“Is it truly the one?” Fionn whispered.

Finegas nodded. His hands trembled.

## 3. The Fire

They carried the salmon to the dwelling. Finegas was tired from the struggle, and Fionn saw that he could scarcely take his eyes from their catch.

“Prepare it for me,” the poet said. “Cook it carefully. But you must not eat any.”

“I won't,” Fionn promised.

He built a steady fire and placed the fish above the embers. Heat filled the room. The skin tightened and glistened; the air grew rich with smoke and the smell of roasting fish. Fionn turned it slowly, making sure one side did not burn while the other remained raw.

He had never been entrusted with anything so important.

A small blister rose on the salmon's skin. Fionn reached out instinctively to press it down. The heat stung his thumb. He snatched his hand away and put it into his mouth to soothe the burn.

It was the smallest of gestures. There was no thunder, no sudden darkness, no voice proclaiming that a prophecy had come true.

Yet something changed.

The hiss of the fire sharpened. He heard the river beyond the wall and imagined, with startling clarity, its course through the land. A remembered verse returned to him with a meaning he had not grasped before. Questions that had seemed separate now touched one another, like streams joining a single current.

He drew his thumb from his mouth and stared at it.

He was still Fionn: still young, still standing beside the hearth, still uncertain what to do next. But the world seemed wider, as if a door had opened onto a room he had never known existed.

When the salmon was ready, he brought it to Finegas without taking a bite.

The poet looked first at the fish, then at the boy.

“Have you eaten any?”

“No. I promised you.”

Finegas studied his face. “Think carefully.”

“I did not eat it,” Fionn insisted. “I only burned my thumb on the skin, and put it in my mouth.”

The silence that followed seemed longer than all the years Finegas had spent beside the river.

## 4. What Was Given

Finegas lowered his gaze.

He had imagined this moment countless times: the taste of the salmon, the sudden lifting of every veil, the end of not knowing. He had pictured the questions he would finally answer and the poems he would write. Now the salmon lay before him, and the gift had passed elsewhere through an accident no one could undo.

For a moment Fionn thought his master was angry.

“I'm sorry,” he said. “I didn't mean—”

“I know.”

Finegas rose and walked to the doorway. Outside, the Boyne moved on as it always had. Nothing in its current showed that a life had changed.

Fionn waited. He would have accepted a rebuke more easily than the old man's silence.

At last Finegas turned.

“All these years,” he said, “I believed I knew how this story would end.”

“Then I have ruined it.”

The poet shook his head. “No. You have reminded me that a prophecy is not the same thing as a plan.”

He looked at the boy for a long time. There was sorrow in his expression, and Fionn was grateful that he did not try to hide it.

“Eat the salmon,” Finegas said.

“But it was meant for you.”

“I have taught you what I can. This is yours now.”

Fionn sat beside the hearth. He ate, slowly at first, while his master watched the fire. There was no feeling of victory. The gift was wonderful, but he knew the cost of it to the man who had welcomed him.

“Will I know everything?” Fionn asked.

Finegas considered the question. “You may discover many things that other people cannot. That does not mean you will always know what is right.”

Fionn thought of the burn, the river and the seven years his teacher could never recover.

“Then I still have much to learn.”

“Yes,” Finegas said, and smiled. “That is a promising beginning.”

In time Fionn would travel far beyond that bend of the Boyne. Songs would be told of his courage and of the companions who followed him. Whenever he sought hidden knowledge, the story says, he could place his thumb in his mouth and call upon the strange gift he had received.

But on the morning he left Finegas, he was simply a young man standing by a river, trying to find words worthy of farewell.

“Thank you,” he said at last.

Finegas placed a hand on his shoulder. “Ask good questions.”

Fionn set off along the bank. Finegas watched until the boy disappeared among the trees, then returned alone to the house. The Boyne went on flowing.
