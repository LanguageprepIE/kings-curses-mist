## 1. A Pig Among the Hazel Trees

Cian recognised Brian before he made out the faces of his brothers. The three men stood where the road crossed the plain of Muirthemne. A feud had divided their households for so long that few could remember the first wrong.

Cian was travelling alone. Near the hazel trees, pigs were rooting in the earth. He glanced towards the three brothers, drew out his enchanted wand and touched it to his chest. A moment later, another pig was nosing among the herd.

Brian had seen the movement.

“There,” he said.

Iuchar could not tell the animals apart. Brian struck his brothers with the wand and changed them into swift hounds. The herd scattered, squealing through broken branches. The dogs singled out one pig and drove it towards Brian, who met it with his spear.

A human voice rose from the wounded animal.

“Let me take my true shape,” Cian pleaded. “Do not leave me to die as a beast.”

Iuchar and Iucharba stopped. Brian withdrew the spear, and Cian became a man again, trembling on the grass.

“You have made a grave mistake,” Cian said. “Had you killed a pig, you would owe the price of a pig. Now you must answer for a man's life. And my son will know.”

All three knew Lugh, the champion of the Tuatha Dé Danann. Brian looked at their swords, then at the stones scattered along the road.

“None of our weapons need speak of this,” he said.

They killed Cian with stones. When they buried him, the ground repeatedly cast his body up again. At last the earth held him, and they heaped stones over the grave. No one spoke on the journey back. Only Iucharba turned once to look; beneath the hazels, the mound seemed smaller than he had expected.

## 2. The Price of Blood

Lugh found his father's grave by following the road Cian had taken. The earth itself, people said, told him how the killing had happened. He ordered the body raised, covered it with his cloak and stayed beside it while a fitting burial was prepared.

At Tara he gathered the warriors of the Tuatha Dé Danann. Brian, Iuchar and Iucharba stood among them.

“What ought a man to pay,” Lugh asked, “if he has murdered another man's father?”

The answers came quickly. Death, banishment, punishments so cruel that the hall fell silent after they were spoken. At last Brian answered.

“Whatever justice requires.”

“Then justice shall have its payment,” said Lugh. “Three apples, a pig's skin, a spear, two horses and their chariot, seven pigs, a young hound, a cooking-spit and three cries upon a hill.”

Iuchar exchanged a glance with his brothers. An odd list, certainly, but scarcely a death sentence. Brian accepted for them all.

Only then did Lugh name the owners. The apples grew in a guarded garden at the world's eastern edge. The skin could close wounds beyond any healer's power. The spear carried a fire of its own. The horses could run over the sea. The seven pigs lived again after each feast. The hound commanded wild beasts. The spit belonged to women of a hidden island; the hill was guarded by warriors sworn to allow no stranger to cry out there.

Brian's expression had changed before Lugh finished. When the brothers left the hall, none of them smiled.

## 3. The Boat That Knew the Way

Tuireann listened to the terms beside the hearth.

“You accepted before you knew where the treasures lay?”

“We could hardly refuse,” Brian replied.

The old man looked from one son to the next. He had raised them to face warriors and storms; he had never learned how to protect them from what they might do themselves.

Lugh lent them a magical boat called the Wave-Sweeper. No sailor needed to know the course: the craft would find any harbour named by its passengers. They embarked before dawn. The boat slipped from the shore before a single oar had touched the sea.

Iucharba watched the Irish coast fade.

“What if we never come back?”

Brian busied himself with the sail.

“Then let us give the journey some purpose.”

Iuchar said nothing. He could still hear Cian's voice as the wounded pig became a man. As the hills vanished, the slap of water against the hull seemed to leave more room for the memory.

## 4. Birds Above the Golden Garden

High walls surrounded the garden of golden apples. Through one narrow opening the brothers glimpsed the trees. Three fruits gleamed upon the branches like little moons, bright even beneath the midday sun.

“We will never reach the trees through that gate,” Iuchar murmured.

Brian raised his wand and changed them all into hawks. They climbed above the walls, swooped among the branches and seized an apple each.

The guardians saw them flee. They became hunting birds and soared after them, scattering sparks from their wings. Light flashed behind the fugitives. A bolt struck so near Iucharba that he faltered and almost touched the sea.

Brian knew they could not stay in the sky.

He lifted his wand as they fell. Three swans appeared where three hawks had flown, and plunged beneath the waves. Their pursuers circled overhead until the sea grew empty beneath them.

When the brothers surfaced, Iucharba was shaking too hard to speak. Brian opened his hand to show the apples, saved from the garden.

“We have them.”

Iuchar gave no answer. The fruit was said to heal wounds. He wondered how many new wounds the journey would leave behind.

## 5. A Poem for a Pigskin

In Greece, a king kept the healing skin among his dearest treasures. The brothers could not arrive as conquerors. Word was already spreading of three dangerous travellers moving between distant courts.

Brian had another plan.

“We shall be poets.”

Dressed in borrowed clothes, they requested an audience. The king welcomed the diversion and allowed Brian to recite. His poem praised a marvellous skin without asking for it outright. The courtiers smiled. The king did not.

“You have not come here to sing,” he said.

“We have come to ask for what your poem already knows you possess,” Brian answered.

The refusal was immediate. Guards closed around the brothers. Brian drew his sword; overturned cups rang against the floor, and the room filled with shouting.

They escaped with the skin. The king did not survive.

Aboard the Wave-Sweeper, Iuchar watched Brian clean his blade.

“We are paying for one death with more deaths.”

Brian could think of no answer worth speaking.

In Persia the spear was so fierce that it had to be kept in water lest it burn the room around it. Again the brothers attempted the disguise of poets. This court received them with suspicion. Brian used one of the golden apples as a weapon and took the spear by force.

Wrapped in wet cloths, the weapon warmed the bottom of the boat. For three nights Iucharba slept with a bucket of water within reach.

## 6. The King Who Chose to Speak

To reach the enchanted horses and chariot, Brian served at a foreign court as a hired warrior until he could approach them. He stole them during a display and fled down a road that ended at the sea.

The horses did not slow. They galloped over the waves, pulling the chariot across water as though it were hard-packed earth.

Next came Easal's kingdom, home of the seven wondrous pigs. The brothers were met at the harbour by an unarmed man.

“I know your names,” said the king. “I have heard what happened in the other courts.”

Brian rested a hand on his sword.

“Then you know why we have come.”

“That is precisely why I am here. I will not fill my hall with bodies to keep seven pigs.”

Easal gave them the animals. But the hound they still needed belonged to a neighbouring ruler connected to his family. Easal offered to accompany them and negotiate.

That king refused to listen. Brian fought again, and won. Yet when the defeated man stood helpless before him, Brian spared him. Easal spoke once more, and this time the hound was surrendered.

That night, as the seven pigs slept near the ship, Iucharba asked Brian why he had let the king live.

Brian glanced across the camp. Easal was sharing food with the sailors.

“I do not know,” he said. “Once I would not have stopped to wonder.”

## 7. The Forgotten Demands

With most of their prizes aboard, the brothers sailed towards Ireland. They did not know that Lugh had cast a spell to dim their memory. The spit and the three cries slipped from their thoughts as the detail of a dream disappears on waking.

At Tara they set out the apples, skin, spear, horses, chariot, pigs and hound.

Lugh inspected them in silence.

“And the spit?”

Something stirred behind Brian's eyes.

“And the cries upon the hill?” Lugh added.

Iucharba stared at his eldest brother. They had crossed seas and left dead men in lands whose names were difficult even to pronounce.

“The payment is not complete,” Lugh said.

They sailed again.

The island of the spit lay beneath the sea rather than upon it. Brian descended alone under the protection of magic and entered a bright chamber in the deep, where women guarded the strange treasure. They were so astonished by his daring that, after arguing and laughing at him, they allowed him to leave with it.

Only Miodhchaoin's Hill remained.

Its guardian had taught Cian the arts of war. He refused to let the brothers pass. First they fought him, then his three sons. By the end the defenders were dead, but the sons of Tuireann were grievously wounded.

“We have to climb,” Brian whispered.

Iuchar tried to stand and fell. Iucharba could hardly speak. Brian drew them both up, supporting one on each side, and carried them towards the summit.

Their first cry vanished into the wind. The second was weaker. For the third, Brian bent towards Iucharba and begged him to gather whatever strength remained.

They cried together.

The fine had been paid.

## 8. The Last Request

The Wave-Sweeper carried the brothers back to Ireland. Brian held the others upright so that they could see the shore, but neither smiled at its sight.

Tuireann hurried to meet the boat. His joy lasted only until he saw the wounds.

“The skin,” Brian murmured. “Lugh has the healing skin. Ask him.”

Their father went to Tara and begged the champion to lend it. Lugh heard him beside the treasures brought from across the world.

“My father cannot come back,” he said.

Tuireann returned empty-handed. Brian still found the strength to plead with Lugh himself. It made no difference.

He lay down between his brothers. They had travelled beyond the known world; now even the doorway seemed impossibly far away.

Iuchar died first, then Iucharba. Brian remained awake a little longer.

“Is it paid?” he asked his father.

Tuireann brushed his son's hair as he had when the three were children.

“All of it.”

Brian closed his eyes.

Tuireann buried his sons together. Tradition tells that grief soon carried the father to his own death.

Lugh kept the apples, the skin and all the other wonders. Cian's grave stood where it had stood before. Those who told the tale in later years never quite agreed which of its deaths deserved the deepest lament.
