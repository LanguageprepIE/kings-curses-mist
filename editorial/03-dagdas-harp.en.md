## 1. The Keeper of Three Melodies

I once heard an old story told on a rain-soaked evening in Ireland. The storyteller spoke of a battle, a stolen harp and a hall filled with enemies. Yet when the tale was finished, it was the music I remembered. Perhaps that is why the story has travelled so far beyond the hall where it was first imagined.

Long before the towns and roads of modern Ireland, the Tuatha Dé Danann were said to inhabit the island. They were a people of the old legends, gifted in craftsmanship, poetry and magic, and their fortunes were bound to the land itself. Among their greatest figures was the Dagda.

His name is often understood as 'the good god', though the title did not mean that he was always gentle or incapable of anger. He was a mighty, larger-than-life figure: powerful in battle, generous at a feast and possessed of an earthy sense of humour. In stories about him, strength and enchantment were seldom far apart.

Of all the wonders associated with him, one of the strangest was a harp whose music could move an entire gathering to tears, laughter or sleep. The musician who served him was Uaithne. Under his hands the instrument sang, but the Dagda alone could summon it when it was beyond reach.

In those days, the Tuatha Dé Danann were fighting the Fomorians, their formidable rivals. After a terrible battle at Mag Tuired, the victorious warriors came home through the rain. It ought to have been a night for songs.

But there was no music.

The Dagda noticed the silence the moment he entered his hall.

There was the sound of feet crossing the rush-covered floor, the clatter of bowls, the low voices of those returning from battle. Outside, rain slipped from the eaves and gathered in the ruts of the courtyard. All the familiar sounds were there.

Except one.

Beside the great fire stood an empty place where the harp had rested. Now only a pale mark showed against the wall. In the confusion of their retreat, the Fomorians had carried away both the instrument and Uaithne, its keeper.

The Dagda stood for a while before the empty wall.

Lugh found him there. The young champion's cloak was still stained with mud, and a cut on his forehead had scarcely stopped bleeding.

“We have won,” Lugh said, though he sounded too tired to celebrate.

“We have,” said the Dagda.

“Then why do you look as though we have lost?”

The Dagda touched the bare patch of stone. “Because they have carried something of ours away.”

Ogma came in behind them, pulling off a battered glove. When he heard what had happened, he looked at the door.

“Shall I bring the others?”

“No,” replied the Dagda. “Three will be enough.”

Neither Lugh nor Ogma asked him what he intended to do.

## 2. The Feast of the Fomorians

They travelled beneath a clouded sky, crossing wet ground where the tracks of the retreating Fomorians were still visible. Once they passed a broken spear buried in the mud. No one stopped to pick it up.

By evening they reached the stronghold where Bres and his father Elatha were feasting with the survivors. Light leaked through the doorways of a great hall. Within, cups struck tables, voices rose above one another, and someone was telling a story that grew more magnificent each time it was repeated.

Ogma listened from the threshold.

“They do not sound defeated.”

“A feast is a good place to forget defeat,” Lugh said.

The Dagda pushed open the door.

At first the strangers attracted little notice. Then a man near the entrance recognised Lugh, and the talk faltered. A bench scraped across the floor. Farther inside, someone reached for a sword.

The Dagda hardly noticed.

His harp hung high upon the wall, above the heads of the feasters, displayed among captured shields and spears. Uaithne was nowhere to be seen.

There it was: the same curve of polished wood, the same fine strings. Yet in the firelight the instrument looked strangely cold.

Bres rose from his seat.

“You chose a bold hour to visit us.”

“I did not come for your hospitality,” said the Dagda.

His eyes were still fixed on the wall.

Bres followed his gaze and gave a short laugh. “The harp? It belongs to the victors of the taking.”

“Does it?” the Dagda asked.

The warriors nearest him stood. Ogma's hand moved towards his sword, but Lugh caught his arm.

“Wait.”

For the first time, the Dagda raised his voice.

## 3. The Harp Answers

“Come to me, Daur Dá Bláo. Come to me, Cóir Cetharchair!”

The old names rang against the rafters.

Nothing happened.

A Fomorian laughed and lifted his cup. Then a thin, clear note trembled through the hall. The cup stopped halfway to his lips.

A second string answered the first.

The harp shook against the wall. For one breath it seemed to strain there, as though a hand were holding it back. Then it sprang free.

Wood and strings swept through the air. Men threw themselves aside; others were not quick enough. The harp struck those standing in its path, and the crash of falling bodies was followed by the sharper sound of overturned cups.

The instrument came into the Dagda's hands.

He caught it as carefully as a sleeping child.

All around him the Fomorians shouted. Ogma had drawn his sword now; Lugh stood with his back to the doorway, measuring the distance between them and the crowd. There were far too many enemies to fight their way out easily.

The Dagda rested the harp against his shoulder.

His fingers found the strings.

## 4. Three Kinds of Music

The first music was so quiet that the shouting did not stop at once.

It wound beneath the noise of the hall, rising and falling until the words of the angry men sounded distant. A warrior near the fire stared at his hands. Another sank onto a bench and covered his face.

The Dagda was playing the strain of sorrow.

Soon the hall was full of weeping. Some cried openly, with their heads bent; others stood bewildered by tears they had not expected. Even those who still gripped their weapons seemed unable to remember what they meant to do with them.

Ogma leaned towards Lugh. “Is he making them mourn the battle?”

“Perhaps,” Lugh whispered. “Perhaps something older.”

The Dagda let the final note fade. The hall remained still.

Then he changed the tune.

A lively rhythm ran from string to string. It rose, darted, doubled back and surprised the ear. Someone let out a startled chuckle. Another answered it. Within moments laughter broke from every side of the room.

The warriors who had been crying wiped their faces. Cups rattled against the tables as people shook with laughter. One man tried to speak, failed, and laughed harder.

Lugh smiled in spite of himself. Even Ogma's stern mouth twitched.

But the Dagda's hands were moving again.

The third melody was slower than either of the others. It had the steady measure of footsteps coming home and the softness of rain after midnight. The laughter weakened. A man sat down and did not rise. Another laid his head upon folded arms.

The tune of sleep flowed through the hall.

Warriors struggled to keep their eyes open, blinking at the fire as though its flames had grown too bright. Then one by one they surrendered. A sword slid from a loosened hand. Someone murmured a last unfinished word. Bres sank back into his chair, and soon the hall was filled with the breathing of sleeping people.

The Dagda lifted his fingers from the strings.

For a moment the three visitors stood among their enemies, listening.

“Now?” Ogma whispered.

“Now,” said the Dagda.

## 5. The Road Home

They slipped between benches and sleeping bodies, passed through the doorway and stepped into the cool air. The night smelled of wet earth. Somewhere beyond the walls an owl called.

Only when the stronghold had disappeared behind the hills did Ogma speak.

“I would rather face ten armed men than hear that first song again.”

Lugh looked at him in surprise. Ogma had taken part in battles that still troubled the dreams of other warriors.

“Whom did you remember?” Lugh asked.

Ogma walked several paces before answering.

“Someone I had not thought of for years.”

The Dagda said nothing. He carried the harp under his cloak, protecting it from the rain that had begun to fall again.

“And Uaithne?” Lugh asked at last.

The Dagda glanced back towards the distant stronghold. “We will learn what became of him. Tonight, we could not have reached every prisoner in that hall.”

The road bent downhill. Water shone in the hollows between the stones.

At the first ridge, the Dagda stopped and drew the harp from beneath his cloak. One string had loosened in its flight across the feasting hall. He tightened it, tested the note, and listened until the sound settled.

Then he played a short, quiet phrase.

Neither Lugh nor Ogma asked which of the three melodies it belonged to. They simply stood beside him on the dark hillside while the last notes went out across the fields.

By morning, the empty space beside the fire would be filled again.

And long after the battle of Mag Tuired, people would remember how the Dagda entered the hall of his enemies and called his harp home.
