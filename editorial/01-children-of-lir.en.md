# Chapter 01 — The Children of Lir

**Status:** Literary text approved by David on 7 November 2026 — website not yet published  
**Edition:** English, first draft (7 November 2026)  
**Collection:** *Of Kings, Curses and Mist*  
**Target audience:** Teenage and general readers  
**Illustrations:** Pilot art requires narrative continuity review

## 1. The House by the Lake

Long before the roads and towns of modern Ireland, when the Tuatha Dé Danann were said to walk the land, there lived a lord named Lir. His home stood among wooded hills, where the wind carried the scent of rain from the lakes.

Lir had once been disappointed in his hopes of becoming king. Yet in time he found a happiness greater than any throne. He married Aobh, and they had four children: Fionnuala, Aodh, Fiachra and Conn.

Fionnuala was the eldest. She watched over her brothers with a patience that sometimes made her seem older than her years. Aodh loved to explore the woods; Fiachra asked questions about everything; and Conn, the youngest, could make the others laugh even on the greyest day.

Their father delighted in them. After long days away, he would return to find them waiting on the path. When winter storms shook the roof, they gathered close to the hearth and listened to stories of ancient Ireland.

Then Aobh died, and the warmth of the house changed. Lir grieved deeply, while Fionnuala tried to comfort her brothers. They still loved the lake, the woods and their games, but every familiar place seemed to hold a memory.

## 2. Aoife's Shadow

In time, Lir married Aoife, Aobh's sister. At first Aoife was kind to the children. She braided Fionnuala's hair and listened to the boys describe their adventures. Lir hoped their home might become joyful again.

But wherever they went, the children were welcomed. Their father hurried to greet them; visitors praised their singing; and the household gathered around them. Aoife began to feel that there was no room in Lir's heart for her.

Jealousy is a quiet thing at first. Aoife kept her bitterness hidden, then fed it with every imagined slight. She knew the children had done her no harm. Still, she came to blame them for her loneliness.

One morning she announced that she would take them to visit their grandfather, Bodb Dearg. Lir agreed, pleased that they would enjoy a journey together.

The road passed Lough Derravaragh, a broad lake where clouds moved across the surface like shadows. Aoife stopped their horses and suggested that the children swim.

The four ran laughing into the water.

When they were beyond the shore, Aoife raised a wand and spoke a spell.

The laughter stopped. White feathers spread where arms had been. Four long necks rose from the ripples. The children had become swans.

For a moment, there was only the sound of water against the reeds.

Then Fionnuala spoke. She still possessed a human voice.

“Why have you done this, Aoife? We never wished you harm.”

Aoife looked away. She could not undo what she had done. She declared that they must remain swans for nine hundred years: three hundred on Lough Derravaragh, three hundred on the Sea of Moyle, and three hundred on the waters near Erris in the west.

The spell would end only when a man from the North and a woman from the South were joined, she foretold. Until then, no one could free them. Yet she left them their memories, their speech and a gift: their singing would be more beautiful than any song heard in Ireland.

It was a cruel comfort.

## 3. The Song of Derravaragh

News of the children's disappearance reached Lir. He searched the road and came at last to the lake. Four white swans glided towards him.

“Father,” called Fionnuala.

Lir knew her voice at once. He stepped into the shallows, reaching towards them, but the spell would not let them return to his home as children.

He stayed beside the lake that night. They told him what had happened, and when words failed, they sang.

Their song floated over the dark water. Travellers stopped to listen. Even those who did not know the story felt its sadness and beauty.

For three hundred years the four swans remained at Derravaragh. Seasons came and went. Trees grew tall and fell. The faces of those who visited changed until no one remained who remembered their childhood.

Through it all, Fionnuala kept the family close. When winds rose, she drew her brothers beside her. When the lake lay still beneath the stars, they sang of their father and the home they could no longer reach.

At last, the first three hundred years ended.

They rose into the sky and flew north.

## 4. The Wild Sea of Moyle

The Sea of Moyle lay between Ireland and Scotland, and it offered none of the shelter of the lake. Its waves climbed like walls. Rain struck the swans' feathers, and bitter winter winds drove them apart.

Once, a storm scattered all four across the dark water. Fionnuala searched through the night, calling their names into the roar of the sea.

At dawn, she found Aodh exhausted against a rock. Later she heard Fiachra's weak reply, and at last Conn appeared between the waves.

They gathered together, trembling but alive.

After that, whenever storms came, the siblings promised to find one another again. They had no power to break the enchantment, but they could still choose how to care for each other.

Years passed. Sailors sometimes heard their strange, beautiful music and wondered whether the voices came from birds, spirits or the sea itself.

When the second three hundred years were over, the swans turned west towards the Atlantic coast.

## 5. The Empty Home

The waters around Erris were lonely, but the siblings found places to rest among the islands. Their final centuries passed beneath changing skies.

Eventually they returned to the land where Lir had lived. They had dreamed of the familiar house, the path between the trees, and their father waiting at the door.

There was nothing left.

Grass covered the old walls. Nettles grew where the hearth had been. The house had vanished into the earth, and all the people they had known were gone.

For the first time, the nine hundred years felt heavier than their wings.

Fionnuala looked at the ruins and understood that the world they remembered could never be restored.

“Then we must carry it with us,” she told her brothers.

And so they flew back towards the western sea.

## 6. The Last Song

On the island of Inis Gluaire, the swans heard a sound unlike any they remembered: a bell ringing from a small Christian settlement.

A holy man named Mochaomhóg heard their song and welcomed them. He listened as Fionnuala told him their long story. In his care they found peace at last.

By then, the prophecy had come true: Lairgnen of Connacht had married Deoch of Munster. When Deoch heard of the extraordinary swans, she begged her husband to bring them to her. Mochaomhóg refused to surrender them.

Angry at being denied, Lairgnen came to the island himself. He seized the four swans, two in each hand. At once their feathers fell away. Before his astonished eyes stood not the children who had entered the lake, but four people aged by nine hundred years.

Lairgnen fled. The holy man stayed beside the siblings, whose journey was ending. In the traditional tale he baptised them before they died, and buried them together, as Fionnuala requested.

Fionnuala had kept her promise to her brothers through wind, hunger, storms and loss. No king or spell had saved them from every sorrow. What endured was the bond they had refused to abandon.

And long after the last notes faded over the island, Ireland remembered the song of the Children of Lir.

## Editorial notes — not part of the story

- This is a **new prose adaptation**, not a reproduction of a modern retelling.
- The childhood personalities, dialogue, sensory descriptions and Fionnuala's final reflection are creative additions.
- Core traditional elements retained: Lir, Aobh, Aoife, four named children, Bodb Dearg, Lough Derravaragh, three periods of three hundred years, the Sea of Moyle, Erris, Inis Gluaire, the Christian bell, return to human form and death.
- The prophecy of Lairgnen and Deoch and Lairgnen's seizure of the birds are included in compressed form; the silver chains and other details are omitted to maintain the target reading length.
- The first illustration depicts exactly four swans. The transformation pilot appears to depict too many bodies in transition and should not be treated as final.
- The family illustration depicts Lir with Fionnuala, Aodh, Fiachra and Conn; ages, faces, attire and colour palette are **artistic choices**, not descriptions claimed from the historical sources.
